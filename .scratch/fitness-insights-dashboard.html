The Big Shift

From century rides to the daily commute

2015 through 2019 was the era of the long ride: Fletcher Flyer, Bike MS, Gran Fondo NY, Tour to Tanglewood — organized century and charity rides regularly topping 100 km, several past 160 km. Riding averaged 309 rides a year at 11.4 km each. Two things happened at once in 2020. The data shows a complete stop from April through June — zero rides for three months, the only gap like it in eleven years — as commuting stopped being necessary at all. And separately, chronic low back pain set in: initially diagnosed as a bulging disk pressing on the sciatic nerve, unresolved by physical therapy or steroid injections. Riding didn't stop; it changed shape. Long training and charity rides gave way to short, strictly-commute riding, the only kind that didn't aggravate it. The longest ride of the year falls from 95 km (2019) to 19 km (2020) and doesn't climb back above 21 km for six years. Every year since has averaged 154 rides at 7.9 km each — half the frequency, about a third shorter — not a decline so much as a six-year adaptation to pain that, at the time, had no fix. A Garmin Edge 810 (2015–2022) was later replaced by a newer Edge model — the 2020 break happens two years before that swap, so it isn't a device artifact.

Daily Pattern

A ride that runs on a work schedule

Every FIT timestamp in this export is recorded in UTC with no stored offset — correcting to US Eastern (UTC−5) turns a scattered-looking distribution into a clean two-hump commute pattern: a morning cluster from 5–8am (1,341 rides) and an afternoon cluster from 3–6pm (1,074 rides). Weekday rides (Mon–Thu) average 6.7 km; weekend rides average roughly 7× longer at about 50 km — the last echo of the long-ride habit, now confined to Saturdays and Sundays.

Reading The Trend Carefully

Slower pace, shorter rides — not necessarily less fit

Average cadence (from real per-second telemetry, not session summaries) fell from about 77 rpm in 2015–2017 to 60 rpm by 2023–2026. Average speed fell alongside it, from 26.5 km/h to roughly 21 km/h. It's tempting to read that as declining fitness, but it lines up exactly with the shift in the section above: open-road century-pace training rides and short, pain-conscious commuting have inherently different pace profiles — riding easier was, for years, part of how the back pain was managed. A trend line across both eras mostly measures which kind of riding was happening and why, not fitness on its own.

A Different Kind of Ride

Peloton, in bursts — not a steady habit

Peloton first shows up in 2021 — 49 rides, averaging 217 kJ over 23 minutes, full classes taken during the pandemic. Then it goes almost silent: 2022–2025 combined add up to just 8 rides. It picks back up sharply in 2026, for a reason the data can pin down almost to the day — see Recovery, next. Peloton sometimes splits a class into separate synced activities — a warm-up, the main set, and a cool-down as their own files. Grouping rides less than 15 minutes apart as one session puts the real count closer to 78 sessions than 92 separate workouts.

The Turning Point

A new diagnosis, and the first pain-free days in years

In May 2026, a new spine specialist changed the diagnosis: not a bulging disk, but spinal stenosis inflaming a specific nerve root. A targeted steroid injection on June 4, 2026 brought complete relief — for the first time in years. The data shows almost exactly when the new routine started. Peloton rides resume June 28, 24 days after the injection, and not with the easy sessions from 2021 — HIIT and Tabata classes from the first week back. A trainer-led strength program appears in the export for the first time ever, starting July 5: Week 1 Day 1, Week 1 Day 2, Week 3 Day 1… twelve sessions in four weeks, the only strength training logged in eleven years of data. Outdoor commuting continues unchanged alongside both — still short, still frequent — now one part of three instead of the only option. Strength sessions come from Strava's own activity log, not this project's FIT/TCX pipeline — Hevy-logged workouts like these were never exported with an underlying GPS/sensor file (confirmed back in this project's very first data-ingestion issue), so there's no heart rate or per-set detail to chart here yet.

    About this data. Built from 2,720 processed activities (2,628 Garmin FIT files, 92 Peloton TCX files) via this project's ingestion pipeline — summary fields from data/processed/*.json, per-second heart rate/cadence/speed/power from data/processed_tracks/*.parquet, queried with DuckDB. The medical/life context in the Recovery section and timeline above is hand-recorded, structured, human-provided context (not device data) from data/context/life_events.json, validated against data/schema/life_event.schema.json — see that file for anything added since this snapshot was built.

    Hour-of-day and weekday figures assume US Eastern time (UTC−5); the source data has no stored timezone offset, so this is an approximation that may drift a little around DST boundaries. Cadence/speed/HR trends reflect the mix of ride types in each period as much as fitness. 8 GPX-sourced activities (2016–2023) were intentionally excluded — no calorie, heart-rate, or power data of their own, and statistically negligible against 2,720 processed rides.
